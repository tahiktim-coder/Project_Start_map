# Silent Exodus — story brief for a writing partner

*A self-contained brief. You do not need any other file. Written 3 October 2026.*

I am a solo developer making a short narrative browser game called **Silent Exodus**. The game itself works: the map, the
crew, the minigames, six sectors and an ending. The **story** is what I need help with. Parts of it are strong, parts
contradict each other, and the central mystery has no satisfying answer yet. Please read everything below before suggesting
anything, and respect the constraints in section 10. Section 12 says exactly what I am asking for.

---

## 1. The game in one paragraph

You are the commander of **EXODUS-9**, a colony ship. Your crew of four wakes after 61 years asleep. Earth's orders: fly down
a fixed heading, find a planet people can live on, settle it. The game is a sequence of six **sectors**. In each one you see a
small star map, have two or three **stops**, scan planets, send an away team down (a short lander minigame), board dead ships,
make choices on short story cards, and then **jump** to the next sector. Energy moves the ship and rations feed the crew. Along
the way you find the wrecks of the ships that went before you, and one **found page** per sector (a log, a memo, a ledger).
At the end of the heading is the finale. A run should take **30–40 minutes**. The art is pixel art with ordered dithering.
The player character never speaks.

## 2. Tone and references

Grounded, lonely, sad, and quietly frightening. Never horror-movie loud. References for tone: *Moon* (2009), *2001*, *Alien*'s
quiet parts, *Interstellar*, *Solaris*, *Outer Wilds*, *Return of the Obra Dinn*, *Firewatch*, *SOMA*, *FTL*. Players should
work things out from objects they find, not from characters explaining.

Colour idea for the whole game (it helps to know the mood): **the universe is cold; only people and the lie are warm.**

## 3. How the game talks (hard rules)

Players already rejected an earlier draft as "AI gibberish", so these rules are strict:

1. Plain, natural speech. One idea per line, 20 words at most. Characters talk like people in a grounded sci-fi film.
2. No pet names, no verbal tics, no catchphrases, no riddles, no invented jargon ("distress call", not "hail").
3. Numbers said plainly ("hull number 980", "about a hundred years").
4. Dead crew never speak.
5. At most about 40 words on screen at once; one line of dialogue at a time.
6. The story is shown through **objects and play** (pages, wreck numbers, minigames), never by a character explaining the twist.
7. **A.U.R.A.**, the ship's computer, is calm, polite and exact, calls the player "Commander", and is never sinister or
   glitchy. Every number she gives is correct, **except one: she always says there are four crew. There are five.**

## 4. The cast

| | who | what they want |
|---|---|---|
| **Cora Moon**, commander (the player) | never speaks | — |
| **Jaxon Mercer**, engineer, 42 | tired, warm, practical; left a daughter on Earth; records letters to her he cannot send | to stop somewhere good and live |
| **Kael Vance**, security, 45 | blunt, suspicious of A.U.R.A.; worked at the shipyard before launch and saw more ships being built than anyone admitted | the truth, and to be believed |
| **Aris Novak**, doctor, 38 | the kindest person aboard; keeps a written list of every dead crew member they find, and reads the names aloud | to understand what happened to them |
| **Mira Chen**, scientist, 29 | young, curious, trusts A.U.R.A. and talks to her like a friend | to be told what to do |
| **A.U.R.A.** | the ship's computer | to keep the crew moving |

Each crew member has three small "moments" during a run where you can side with them or not. Backing someone twice unlocks
their ending (section 7).

## 5. What the crew is told at the start (the lie)

The title screen says: *"Eight went before you. None of them called home."*

The opening film: *"Thousands of ships. Every one on its own heading. On yours, eight went first. You are the ninth."*

The wake-up scene:

> Cold air and bright lights. After sixty-one years asleep, the ship has woken all five of you.
>
> **A.U.R.A.:** Good morning, Commander. All four crew are awake and well. The ship is in one piece.
> **A.U.R.A.:** Your orders have not changed. Find a planet people can live on, and settle it.
> **A.U.R.A.:** Earth is sending ships in every direction. Eight went this way before us. We are the ninth.
> **Jaxon:** Eight ships ahead of us. I hope they left us a good planet.
> **Vance:** Eight ships, and not one of them ever sent a message home?

Shortly after, "the head count" scene: Vance points out there are five people aboard and she keeps saying four. Asked to list
them, A.U.R.A. names all five ("Jaxon Mercer. Aris Novak. Kael Vance. Mira Chen. And you, Commander.") and then says "Four
crew." She does not notice. Jaxon: "It's a glitch. She slept sixty years too. Let it go." **I love this beat. It must stay.**
What it *means* is the biggest open question (section 12).

## 6. What is true in the current version (the canon as built)

**One heading.** Every ship Earth ever built was sent down this same heading. Nothing went anywhere else.

**The drive throws ships back in time.** It moves a ship forward in space and backward in time. Later ships were thrown
further back. So the deeper you go, the **higher** the hull number on the wreck and the **older** the wreck:

| sector | hull numbers of the wrecks | dead for about |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | EXODUS-1 to 8 | 20 years |
| 3 | 212 to 980 | 100 years |
| 4 | 1,400 to 6,000 | 200 years |
| 5 | 9,000 to 22,000 | 300 years |
| 6 | 30,000 to 41,000 | 400 years |

EXODUS-9 arrives **last**, after everything Earth ever sent has already failed. Nobody goes home: the drive only goes one way.

**The light at the end.** At the end of the heading is a light that looks like a sun, the warm home star you were sent to
find. It is not a sun. It gives no heat. It **reads** whatever reaches it, completely, and what it has fully read it can make
again (the deep sectors contain its copies: ghost planets, wrong places, voices on dead channels). It does not chase anyone.
It is patient. Dead hulls drift across its face.

**The disc.** In 1977 Earth launched a probe carrying a gold record (the real Voyager Golden Record). On its cover is a map:
fourteen lines from our Sun to fourteen pulsars. That map tells anyone who finds it **where** Earth is, and **when** the disc
was made (pulsars slow down at known rates). In the current canon the disc drifted into the light, the light is reading it,
and Earth's secret aim is to reach the disc and destroy the map before it is fully read.

**A.U.R.A. has a twin.** Every ship's computer is half of a linked pair; the other half sits in a vault on Earth. The link
carries *state, not words*, and does not care about distance or *when*. Years after the first ship left, its twin on Earth
began reciting the disc in a voice that was not its own: something out there had the disc and was reading up the link toward
Earth. That is how Earth found out. "Hull 9's twin is the only one in the vault that has never changed."

**Why crews are lied to (current version).** Whatever a crew knows, the light learns. The first crews were told the truth and
stopped flying. So Earth sends crews who believe the cover story.

alive in pods (living people are only ever found asleep in pods); crew moments (Jaxon's daughter, Aris's list of the dead,
Vance's shipyard story, Mira and A.U.R.A.).

## 7. The endings as built now

At the light, A.U.R.A. is read first and says, for the first time, "Five crew, Commander." Then you choose. You can only pick
an ending whose crew member you backed twice during the run:

| ending | whose | what happens |
|---|---|---|
| **Destroy the map** | Vance | you go out alone and cut the map off the disc with a torch: it knows what we are, never where |
| **Let Aris go to it** | Aris | she walks into the light reading her list of names aloud, choosing to be read |
| **Take the disc and keep flying** | always open | you never stop, so it can never finish reading the map |
| **Let A.U.R.A. decide** | Mira | she burns the ship, the disc and herself, as her real orders said |
| **Wake the sleepers** | only if you carried pods | people from far later ships wake, are not surprised, and vote |
| **Turn around and settle** | Jaxon | a small cold world in sight of the light; Jaxon finishes a letter to his daughter |

Each ending closes on a line about the twin computer in the vault on Earth. You can also settle a planet early (a colony
ending written from about 100 variations), accept a perfect copy of a world in an anomaly ("the Wrong Place"), or lose
(all hands lost, hull breach, command failure, A.U.R.A. mutiny, stranded).

## 8. Minigames that carry the story (built, approved)

The story must work through these, since they are what players liked most:

- **Fly the corridor.** Every jump between sectors is a short flight down the heading. A counter of **ship beacons heard**
  climbs (8 in sector 2, hundreds in 3, thousands in 4, tens of thousands in 5, about forty thousand in 6), but you only ever
  **see** one or two hulls up close per jump. The dread is the counter against the empty dark.
- **Date it with the disc.** At a wreck, you lay the wreck's last star reading (its pulsar measurements on the day it died)
  over the disc's pulsar map. Two controls: **years** (how much the pulsars have slowed) and **distance** (how far the angles
  have shifted). When all fourteen lines match you know **when and where** that ship died, and every dated wreck stays plotted
  on a chart. The chart shows the pattern by itself: the further down the heading, the older the dead. Dating a wreck also
  reveals that sector's story planet (where that wreck's crew was heading).
- **The cutting torch.** You cut open a wreck's hatch by hand (heat, fuel, a satisfying metal crack). The same torch is used
  on the disc in the "Destroy the map" ending.
- **Seal the breach.** A meteor hit leaks air; you find it by watching dust drift and decide whether to shut someone in.
- **Planned, waiting on the story:** **the walk out** (the finale played: drift on a tether from the ship to the disc while
  the light's sweeps read whatever they touch and the crew's voices drop off the radio one by one) and **dead channels**
  (tune an old radio and hear dead ships, including a 400-year-old ship's computer saying "Four crew, Commander. All accounted
  for." in A.U.R.A.'s exact voice).

## 9. What is wrong with the story right now (honest list)

1. **The count (four vs five) has no good answer.** It is the best hook in the game and it currently points at nothing.
2. **"Why us?"** Why can the last, weakest ship do what 41,000 ships could not? Nothing answers this.
3. **The time throw contradicts itself.** "Each new hull is thrown further back" would put hull 9 in front of hulls 1–8, yet
   hulls 1–8 died twenty years before we arrive and "EXODUS-9 arrives last". Both cannot be true.
4. **The disc does not register in play** until the very end, and the 1977 Voyager disc physically cannot be at the end of
   the heading (Voyager moves about 17 km per second and is still close to the Sun).
5. **The big reveal lands twice.** The sector 2 tape and Vance already say "hundreds" before the throw shows it.
6. **The light has no teeth.** The game never shows what being read costs anyone.
7. **The twins on Earth are over-explained and under-felt;** every ending closing on a vault line is too much.
8. **The six endings are six paragraphs.** They would be stronger as answers to one question.
9. **Leftovers of a rejected answer** still sit in the game: the crew plate's blank fifth panel, the ledger's empty commander
   column, the sector 6 log's "it cannot find me".

## 10. Constraints and things already rejected (please do not suggest these)

**Must stay:** the head-count beat (she names five and says four, and does not notice); A.U.R.A. calm and exact, never a
villain; one heading; the light that looks like a sun; **one disc only** (the real 1977 record); the six sectors; the crew
and their wants; the minigames above; nobody goes home; plain speech.

**Rejected:**
- The count explained as paperwork ("the crew list was sealed on Earth before a commander was chosen"). The designer called it
  suspense with no substance.
- Every Exodus ship carrying its own gold copy of the disc. Rejected: there is only one disc.
- Most of the crew secretly being robots who think they are human (cliché in this genre; also breaks the rule that the light
  reads machines instantly).
- Aliens as a civilisation, an alien broadcast, faster-than-light travel home, any ending that says "you are home".
- Dead all along, simulation, clone, ghost, amnesia, it-was-a-dream, time-loop-reset as the main twist.
- A HUD meter showing how much the light has "read".
- Characters explaining the twist out loud.

## 11. The idea on the table (the designer's own, not decided)

**A bootstrap paradox.** Long after us, an Exodus crew worked out everything and sent a warning back to Earth, back in time:
*"Send zero Exodus ships here. Do not send anyone to [these coordinates]."* It arrived broken. Earth read the fragments as an
instruction: *send Exodus ships to this place.* A message from the future was so enormous that governments rebuilt themselves
around it. A fragment about the disc was read as "the disc should not have been sent" (too late, it was sent in 1977), or
perhaps as "send ships toward the thing called the disc". So Earth built Exodus 1, then 2, and kept going, each ship sending
back a little information before going silent. The warning caused the very programme it warned against. We are ship 9.

**The designer's latest thoughts (unresolved, they felt a bit lost here):**
- One disc only. Earth sent it.
- The broken message reached Earth in **2029**, rushed and torn by time and distance. Help is wanted with its wording: what
  the full warning said, and which fragments survived so that it reads as the opposite.
- Every Exodus follows the **same precalculated route of planets**, worked out from past data. That is why our crew keeps
  finding the earlier ships, at planets and during jumps.
- **The earlier crews saw the graveyard too**; they just died in earlier sectors and never got as far as we do.
- The open question that matters most: **why are WE the ones who send the warning?** "We arrive last" is not good enough.

**Suggestions made by another assistant (Claude) on top of this, also not decided:**
- Make our crew the senders, so the loop closes in the player's hands.
- The warning travels through A.U.R.A.'s twin link (state, not words, timeless), which is why it arrives in pieces; and every
  ship is a "wire" the light can follow home, which is why the warning says send zero.
- The count: the broken message carried a header, "EXODUS-9 · four crew". A.U.R.A. reads her count down the same timeless
  link, so she reports the ship as it will be when the message is sent. One person will not be aboard at the end: the
  commander walks out to the disc to send it. "Four crew, Commander" was a goodbye from minute one.

**Four earlier answers to the count**, for reference: *One More* (A.U.R.A. took the commander's live pod from the first wreck
because she cannot wake a crew without one; you were that ship's commander); *The Walk* (every commander walked out to the
disc alone and never came back, so "four" is the ship after you leave); *The Blind Spot* (A.U.R.A. hid you from the light
and erased her memory of doing it; judged a cliché); *The Script* (A.U.R.A. is being overwritten by 41,000 dead ship
computers that all ended on "four crew, all accounted for").

## 12. What I am asking you for

Please help me make one story that holds together. Specifically:

1. **Why us?** Why is EXODUS-9 the crew who understands and sends the warning (or does whatever the ending is), when the
   crews before them saw the graveyard too?
2. **The disc.** What is the disc's role in the paradox version, and how is the one 1977 disc at the end of the heading, given
   it physically cannot have travelled there? (Or does it not need to be there?)
3. **The broken message.** Write the full warning and the fragments that survived, so that Earth's misreading is believable
   and the player can later rebuild the true message. Keep it short and plain.
4. **The count.** One answer to "she names five and says four" that pays off emotionally and fits the paradox, without the
   rejected answers in section 10.
5. **The time rule.** Fix the contradiction in section 9, point 3, simply.
6. **The ladder.** For each of the six sectors: the one question the player is holding, and the one object or event that
   sharpens it. Reuse the found pages, story planets and minigames where you can; say which pages need rewriting.
7. **The ending as one question** with five or six answers, each closing on A.U.R.A.'s final count.

**How to answer:** give **at most two or three options**, not ten. For each option: a one-line pitch; the truth in plain words;
the sector-by-sector ladder (one line per sector); what the player *does* at the end; what changes in the existing game (which
pages, scenes or lines); and the biggest risk. Say plainly which option you would choose and why. Keep every proposed line of
dialogue under 20 words and in plain speech.
