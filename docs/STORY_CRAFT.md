# How great writers would solve our mystery (2026-10-10)

**What this is.** Rules from writers and games that solved mysteries like ours, each turned into a test for *Silent Exodus*.
It does not pick the story; it is the yardstick for whichever spine we pick. The ten tests at the end are the short version.

**The one-paragraph lesson.** Every source below says the same thing in a different way: a mystery is good when its answer
is *smaller and closer* than the question. "Why are all these ships dead?" should end at "because of something about us,
on this ship". Pile on questions and the player stops caring (*Lost*). Answer with something they already saw, that hurts
someone they know, and they replay the whole game in their head (*Arrival*, *Obra Dinn*, *Solaris*).

## 1. Plot moves on "but" and "therefore", never "and"

- **Rule.** If two scenes join with "and then", the second one is filler. Join them with "but" or "therefore".
- **From.** Trey Parker and Matt Stone (*South Park*), visiting a writing class at NYU in 2011.
- **For us.** Today our six sectors join with "and": a crew plate AND a memo AND a log AND orders AND a ledger AND a log.
  Each page explains; none changes what the crew does next. Write each sector as one sentence and join the six. Example of
  the shape (built from today's pieces, not a decision): *We find the eight wrecks we were told about, BUT A.U.R.A. says four
  crew. THEREFORE Vance starts checking her. BUT the next wreck has a higher number than ours, THEREFORE we date it, BUT it
  is a hundred years dead…* If a sector cannot start with "but" or "therefore", it has no job yet.

## 2. Play fair: every clue on the table before the answer

- **Rule.** The player must have seen every piece of the answer before it is given. No new fact, power or person at the end.
- **From.** Ronald Knox's ten rules for detective stories (1929): the culprit appears early; no accident or hunch helps the
  detective; every clue the detective sees, the reader sees. And rule ten: **"twins, and doubles generally, must not appear
  unless we have been duly prepared for them."**
- **For us.** (a) The light is our culprit, and it is on screen from minute one: it is the heading. Good. (b) Whatever the
  light does to people at the end, it must have done visibly earlier, to someone else. (c) Knox's rule ten is about our
  sector 5 twin, the ship exactly like ours. It needs a setup first: sector 4, *The Garden*, is where the light's copies
  (ghost planets, the Wrong Place) should be seen, so the twin lands as dread, not as a trick.

## 3. Tell only the truth, and still mislead

- **Rule.** The most famous fair mystery hides the answer in a narrator who never lies, only leaves things out.
- **From.** Agatha Christie, *The Murder of Roger Ackroyd* (1926). Readers were furious and then admitted every line was true.
- **For us.** A.U.R.A. is our Ackroyd narrator. Every number she gives is right except one, always the same way, and she
  never dodges a question. The moment she is sloppy anywhere else, players call it a bug and stop treating her as evidence.

## 4. One scene, one deduction, confirmed in threes

- **Rule.** Each scene asks one question the player can answer from what is in front of them. Confirm answers in groups, so
  guessing does not pay and working it out does.
- **From.** *Return of the Obra Dinn* (Lucas Pope, 2018): each frozen memory is one death; you write the answer in the book
  yourself; the game confirms fates only three at a time.
- **For us.** Six sectors, six deductions. Dating a wreck with the disc is already our Obra Dinn book: the player does the
  work, and the chart shows the pattern without anyone saying it. For the count, never confirm on one clue: three objects
  that agree (for example a wreck, a dead channel, a ledger line) and then the crew reacts. Nobody explains.
- **Two and two.** Billy Wilder's rule, which Andrew Stanton (Pixar, TED 2012) calls "the unifying theory of two plus two":
  give the parts, not the sum. The page line "we were sent less far back in time" does the sum for the player: cut it.

## 5. The mystery box is a promise, not a plot

- **Rule.** A sealed question is only worth opening if what is inside is smaller, closer and sharper than the box. Never add
  a mystery without closing one.
- **From.** J.J. Abrams, "The mystery box" (TED, 2007), and *Lost* (2004–2010), where questions piled up and the answers,
  when they came, cost nobody anything.
- **For us.** We are carrying about six boxes: the light, the 2029 message, what Earth hid, the count, the twin ship, why us.
  That is the *Lost* road. The designer's instinct ("should it tie in to the main quest?") is exactly the cure: each one must
  turn out to be a face of the same answer. Any box that does not, cut it or leave it as background.

## 6. The answer should cut deeper than the question

- **Rule.** The best reveals are not "here is how the machine works" but "it was about you all along".
- **From.** *Firewatch* (the conspiracy is a small human grief that mirrors the hero's own); Sophocles' *Oedipus* (the
  detective is the cause); *Outer Wilds* (the thing you chased is not a weapon or a god).
- **For us.** "Why four?" must end on a person aboard, and cost one of them something. Paperwork failed this test (rejected
  2026-09-25). "The computer is broken" fails it too. Example of an answer that cuts deeper (illustration only): if the one
  thing the light lacks is *where* Earth is, then the dating chart the player builds, wreck by wreck, a map of where and when,
  is the most dangerous object on the ship. The player's own work becomes the threat, and the one disc finally matters in play.

## 7. An alien mind can stay unexplained if its rules never break

- **Rule.** Do not explain what it is. Do keep what it does exactly the same every time, so the player can predict it.
- **From.** Stanislaw Lem, *Solaris* (1961). The ocean reads the scientists and makes living copies of the people in their
  memories (Kelvin's dead wife). Nobody ever learns why; a whole library of theories fails. It still never cheats.
- **For us.** Write the light as three rules that hold everywhere: it reads whatever reaches it (machines fast, people
  slowly); what it has fully read, it can make again; it gives no heat and never moves. Its motive may stay unknown. Its
  behaviour may not. The designer's "deceiver" works best Lem's way: it never lies or speaks; it simply *looks like* what Earth
  hoped to find, a warm home star.

## 8. Copies: show what is lost, never make the player the copy

- **Rule.** A copy is frightening for what the original loses, not for the surprise. Show the cost on someone else first.
- **From.** *SOMA* (Frictional, 2015): every copy is a coin toss and you are always the one left behind. *Annihilation*
  (VanderMeer 2014; Garland's film 2018): the double walks home, the original does not. Also in the novel: the lighthouse
  holds hundreds of journals, far more than the twelve expeditions the team was told about. That is our hull count exactly.
- **For us.** "Being read" needs one visible, plain cost, shown on the twin ship or a wreck before it can happen to us (rule 2).
  Warning: "you were the copy all along" is on our rejected list (clone twist). Copies belong to the light's behaviour, not to
  the player's identity.

## 9. Knowing the ending can be the most moving part

- **Rule.** A time loop is moving when someone chooses it knowing how it ends. It is a puzzle when nobody chooses.
- **From.** Ted Chiang, *Story of Your Life* (1998), and *Arrival* (2016): Louise knows her daughter will die and chooses her
  anyway. *Outer Wilds* (2019): knowledge is the only thing you keep, and the end cannot be stopped, only met.
- **For us.** On "did we send the message?": yes only if sending it is a real choice with a cost, made by a player who has
  worked out what it will cause. If every ending sends it automatically, it is a cutscene. Keep the time part to one picture
  and one object; Outer Wilds never puts physics on screen.

## 10. The twist re-reads the opening

- **Rule.** The best twist adds almost nothing new. It makes the player see the first scene again with different eyes.
- **From.** *Arrival*: the "memories" in the opening were the future. *The Sixth Sense*: only the boy ever talks to Malcolm.
- **For us.** The designer's new opening (the grand launch, the crew on the podium, a TV reportage from the crowd) is the
  perfect place for this. Players watch it before they know what to look for. At least one image or line in it (a
  headline, a banner, a number on the podium) should only make sense at the light. Same footage, replayed at the end.

## 11. Story through fragments: each one a real object for someone else

- **Rule.** A found document works when it has a real reason to exist and is written to its own reader, not to the player.
  Repeated words across fragments carry the meaning.
- **From.** *Signalis* (rose-engine, 2022): you find copy after copy of the same model of person, and one repeated promise
  holds the whole story together. *Kentucky Route Zero* (2013–2020): never explains its magic; your choices decide how
  people feel and remember, not just what happens.
- **For us.** Every page must only know what its writer could know (the 2026-10-04 audit found pages that know the future).
  Our repeated phrase already exists: "Four crew, Commander. All accounted for." It should come back from places the player
  does not expect, and mean more each time.

## 12. Write all of it, show an eighth

- **Rule.** You may leave things unsaid only if you know them. Readers feel the difference.
- **From.** Hemingway's iceberg (*Death in the Afternoon*, 1932): an iceberg moves with dignity because only an eighth shows.
- **For us.** The designer asked "maybe keep it unknown?" Yes, on screen. No, in the doc. The spine must answer every question
  (the 2029 wording, what the light is for, why the count) even where the game never will. Unknown and undecided feel different.

## 13. Every gun on the wall fires

- **Rule.** Anything the story points at must pay off; anything that cannot pay off must come off the wall.
- **From.** Anton Chekhov's letters (1889). Pixar's story rules (Emma Coats, 2011), number 19: coincidences that get heroes
  *into* trouble are fine; ones that get them *out* are cheating.
- **For us.** On the wall now: the disc, the count, Jaxon's planned "ten gets the new drive", the cutting torch, the sleepers, Jaxon's
  letters, Aris's list of the dead, the twin ship, the vault twins. Each fires or goes. "Why us?" must not be luck (Pixar 19):
  we got further because of something we have or did, which the player can name.

## 14. Endings take something the player chose to love

- **Rule.** An ending hurts when it takes what the player invested in by choice, through the mechanic they used all game.
- **From.** *Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons* (2013): after the older brother dies, you must press his button to go on.
  *SOMA*'s coin toss. *Shadow of the Colossus*: the hero's victories are the harm.
- **For us.** The standing system already ties each ending to a crew member the player backed. Make that person pay, or be
  the one who decides who pays. The loss should come through something the player has done by hand (the tether, the torch,
  the dating chart), and, as already agreed, every irreversible choice gets a crew moment with "Not yet" offered first.

## The designer's ideas of 2026-10-10, run through these rules

| idea | passes if… | fails if… |
|---|---|---|
| The light is a deceiver that wants to know where Earth is | its only act is reading and it *looks* like hope (rule 7); "where" is the one thing it lacks, so the disc's map matters | it talks, lures with words, or hunts ships (no pursuer, settled 2026-09-20) |
| A future crew's message started the programme | it is one message, shown as one object, and sending it costs us something (rules 5, 9) | a second message is added to retrieve the disc: two boxes where one will do |
| What Earth hid ties in to the main quest | the secret *is* a piece of the final answer (for example, what the 2029 message said) | it is a separate conspiracy (rule 5) |
| A.U.R.A. drifts near the light, hence "four" | the error is one exact number, starts at a moment the player can find (the first jump), is seen in other ships' computers, and the number four points at a person (rules 3, 6) | it spreads into general glitches (STYLE rule 9) or "four" has no reason beyond "she is broken" |
| We sent the message | the player chooses to send it, knowing (rule 9) | it happens in every ending regardless |

## The checklist: ten tests any spine must pass

1. **One answer.** The spine fits in two plain sentences a player could repeat after the credits, and the light, the message,
   Earth's secret, the count, the twin and the disc are all faces of that one answer. Any mystery that is not gets cut.
2. **But / therefore.** Written as six sentences, the six sectors join with "but" or "therefore", never "and". Every
   sector's discovery changes what the crew does next.
3. **Fair play.** Every piece of the final answer is shown on screen (an object, a number, a picture) before the light. The
   last scene adds no new fact, power or person. The twin ship is prepared for before sector 5.
4. **One scene, one deduction.** Each sector gives the player one thing to work out from objects, mostly by dating wrecks,
   confirmed by about three agreeing clues. No character explains it; the player is sometimes a sector ahead of the crew.
5. **The answer cuts deeper.** "Why four?" ends on a person aboard and costs one of them something. Paperwork, a glitch or
   "the computer is broken" fails.
6. **Fixed rules, open motive.** The light can be written as three rules that never break, and A.U.R.A. is wrong about one
   number only, always the same way, calmly. Why the light wants anything may stay unknown on screen; the writers know it.
7. **The cost is seen first.** Being read takes something plain and visible from a person, and the player sees it happen to
   someone else (the twin ship, a wreck) before it can happen to us. The player is never revealed to be the copy.
8. **Why us is earned.** We get further than forty thousand ships because of something we have or did that the player can
   name. Not luck, not being chosen.
9. **Every gun fires, and the opening re-reads.** The disc, the count, Jaxon's "ship ten" line, the torch, the sleepers,
   the letters and Aris's list each pay off or are cut; the one disc changes what the player does at the end. At least one
   thing in the launch reportage only makes sense at the light.
10. **The ending takes something chosen.** Every ending costs something the player chose to invest in (a backed crew member,
    a carried sleeper, A.U.R.A.), through a mechanic they have used by hand, after a crew moment. No ending is free, and no
    ending explains everything.
