# Five story questions, in plain words (2026-10-10)

Nothing here is decided and nothing is built. Each question says what it is and why a player would feel it, then gives
three answers and the one I would pick. The opening cannot be redrawn until questions 2 and 5 are answered, because it
shows what Earth told us. The jumps between sectors barely wait on the story: only the ship like ours (question 1) and
A.U.R.A.'s crew count (question 3) change. See OPENING_AND_JUMPS.md.

---

## 1. What does the player do at the light, and what does it cost to be read?

**The question.** The game ends at the light: the thing that looks like a sun, gives no heat, and reads whatever reaches it.
Today the ending is built around the gold disc floating inside it, but we agreed there is only one disc and it cannot be
out there. So the last scene has no object and no action. And the game never shows what being read actually does to a
person. If nobody loses anything, the player has nothing to fear and nothing to choose.

**A. Someone goes out.** The light reads the ship from the outside in, and somebody has to go out on a tether to meet it.
The player picks who goes: the commander alone, Aris (who wants to), or Vance. Or nobody goes, and the ship turns away.
Whoever is read
does not come back as themselves, and the light makes a copy of what it reads. Earlier on the route, the player has
already seen one such copy without knowing it: the ship exactly like ours in sector 5. The game ends on A.U.R.A. counting
the crew one last time.

**B. Send one last call home.** At the light, the ship's transmitter is the only thing still working. The player records a
message to Earth, choosing a few words from what the trip taught them, while the light reads the crew one by one. Being read
costs memory: Mira forgets her mother's name, Jaxon forgets his daughter's face, and they say so. It ends when the call
goes out, and the player sees how little of it will survive the distance.

**C. Wipe the ship's memory.** What the light wants is our computer: every star chart A.U.R.A. ever made points back home.
The player chooses: erase A.U.R.A. (she says goodbye, and we lose the way home), let her be read, or fly on and never stop.
Being read costs the machine, not the people. It ends on the crew alone in a quiet ship, or on A.U.R.A. speaking in
a voice that is no longer quite hers.

**My pick: A.** It is an act the player does with their own hands, it uses the planned minigame of walking out of the ship
on a tether, it gives every crew member's wish a place in the ending, and it ties straight into why A.U.R.A. says four crew
(question 3). B can sit inside A as the last thing the commander does before going out, if question 5 keeps the message.

## 2. What did Earth know when it launched us, and what did it tell us?

**The question.** Today the crew is told "Earth is sending ships in every direction. Eight went this way before us." Later
pages say Earth lied: every ship went the same way, and commanders were told to keep it from their crews. But in the
working story, ships never call home, so Earth could not have known about the graveyard when we left. If Earth knew nothing,
there is no lie. The answer decides the opening film, the tape in sector 2, the orders in sector 4, and the ledger in
sector 5.

**A. Earth knew nothing more than it said.** The briefing was honest. Eight ships really did go before us, and nobody on
Earth knew what was out there. The player feels shared innocence: the horror is finding out together, with no villain.
The tape, the commanders' orders and Vance's shipyard story would be cut or rewritten.

**B. Earth had one secret: why it sent us.** Earth received a strange broken message years before the programme began, and
built every ship around it. It told crews a calmer story (many ships, many directions, find a planet) because "a voice
told us to go there" sounds mad. The player slowly learns the mission was built on something Earth never shared. It ends
with the crew understanding they were sent on a guess, not a plan.

**C. Earth knew ships were dying and kept building.** Each ship's computer had a twin on Earth that kept talking after
launch, so Earth watched crews die and sent more anyway. The commanders' orders say: do not tell your crew. This is the
darkest and most familiar version: the player ends angry at Earth. It also needs a way to send news back home, which
the newer story does not have.

**My pick: B.** It keeps a secret for Vance to dig at, it keeps the sector 4 orders with a smaller lie ("don't tell them
about the message", not "don't tell them they will die"), and it does not need Earth to hear from any ship after launch.

## 3. Why does A.U.R.A. say "four crew" when there are five of us?

**The question.** In the first minutes, A.U.R.A. lists all five people on board by name, then says "Four crew." She does not
notice. (Below, "the count" means this.) This is the best hook in the game, and players remember it. But it currently
points at nothing. An earlier answer (the crew list was printed before a commander was chosen) was rejected as paperwork. The answer has to mean something by
the end, without making A.U.R.A. broken or evil.

**A. You came from the first wreck.** Our commander never made it aboard on Earth. A.U.R.A. cannot wake a crew without one,
so on the way she stopped at the first wreck, found its commander still alive in a sleep pod, and brought her aboard. That
commander is the player. In sector 1, Aris reads out the names of the dead crew you once led, without knowing it.

**B. She counts the ship as it will be at the end of the trip.** By the time the voyage ends, one person is gone, and
she is already counting that. She cannot explain how she knows. The player spends the
game wondering who it will be. This only works if question 5 is answered A (our own message travels back in time).

**C. Every ship lost its commander at the light.** Every Exodus ship that reached the light lost exactly one person there: the commander
who went out alone. Their computers all logged the same last words: "Four crew, Commander. All accounted for." The light
has read those computers, and ours is starting to repeat them. She names five because she can see you; she says four
because every ship before us said four. If nobody goes out at the end, the pattern breaks and she says "Five crew" for
the first time.

**My pick: C.** It works with or without question 5. It needs answer A of question 1 (someone goes out at the light).
It turns the count into the question the ending asks (who goes out), and the player can prove it with objects they find: empty commander lockers in the wrecks, a dead ship's computer saying
our words on an old radio channel.

## 4. When does the player find out why she says "four"?

**The question.** Whatever the answer to question 3, the game has to decide how long the player waits for it. Last time it
was explained in the same scene it appeared, and the mystery died after five minutes. Too late, and players think it is a
bug. This shapes every sector: what clue, if any, each one adds.

**A. One clue per sector, never a speech.** Each sector adds one object: an empty commander's locker (sector 1), a dead
ship's computer still saying "four crew" (sector 3), a dead ship's crew list with five names at launch and a last log entry
that says four (sector 5). Nobody explains it. Most players work it out a sector before the crew does, and the light confirms it.

**B. A pure mystery until the light.** Nothing about the count appears between the first scene and the end. At the light,
one scene answers it all. Simpler to write, but the middle of the game has no thread to hold, and a big answer in one
scene usually feels like a speech.

**C. Answered early, then dreaded.** By the end of sector 2 the player knows someone will be missing at the end, but not
who. The rest of the game is watching the crew and wondering. Strong tension, but there is no surprise left at the light.

**My pick: A.** Players liked working things out from wrecks and numbers; this uses exactly that, and the player gets to
feel clever before the ending tells them they were right.

## 5. Did we send the message that started everything?

**The question.** This is the designer's own idea, paused on 3 October. In 2029 Earth receives a broken message written in
the same star-map language as the gold disc. Earth misreads it as an order to send ships, and the Exodus programme begins.
The twist: the message was sent by us, from the light, as a warning. Only pieces of it arrived, and the pieces said the
opposite. It is a powerful idea, but making it hold together raises hard questions (what year it is for our crew, how the
call travels back, why every ending sends it, whatever the player chose). The game is 30 to 40 minutes, and already carries several mysteries.

**A. Keep the full loop.** At the light the crew sends a warning home. The game shows which pieces will survive the
distance, and the player recognises the message Earth received in 2029. Nothing changes; it was always so. The biggest
"oh" the game could have, and the most work: several pages, the opening film and the ledger dates all change.

**B. Keep the message, never say who sent it.** Earth received a broken message in 2029 and built the programme around it.
The game shows the pieces (the opening film, a page in sector 2) but never says who sent them. At the light, the crew sends
its own call home. Players who pay attention can connect the two; nobody says it.

**C. No message.** Earth saw what looked like a warm sun with planets at the end of the heading and sent ships to settle
there. No time loop, no 2029 message. The jumps still throw ships back in time, and the light is simply what was waiting. Cleanest for a
short game, but it loses the designer's favourite idea and the gold disc's star map loses its reason to matter.

**My pick: B.** It keeps the idea and the disc's star map, it gives the ending a quiet second meaning for players who look
for it, and it avoids putting a time-travel explanation on screen. If the designer wants the loop said out loud, A can be
built on top of B later without undoing anything.

---

## How the picks fit together

Earth received a broken message in 2029 and built every ship around it, without telling the crews why (2, 5). Every ship
that reached the light lost its commander there, and every ship's computer ended its log on "four crew" (3). The player
finds that pattern one object at a time (4). At the light, the player decides who goes out, or whether anyone does; the
crew sends its last call home; A.U.R.A. counts the crew one last time (1).
